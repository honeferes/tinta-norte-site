It's a quarter to ten on a Tuesday night. The dishes are done, the kids are finally quiet, and the water heater in the garage has just made a sound no water heater should make.

Marisol picks up her phone. She doesn't open a directory. She doesn't call her cousin, not yet. She types, with one thumb, the way she'd say it out loud to her sister:

*plomero cerca de mi*

No accent on the *í*. No capital letter. No patience for either.

She could be in Houston or Hartford, in Phoenix or in a small town in Georgia where the only Mexican bakery closes at six. It doesn't matter. What happens next on that small, bright screen happens the same way all across the country, every night, in every city. And most local businesses never find out they were part of it — because they weren't on the screen at all.

Here is how that search really works, and what it asks of you.

## They search in two languages — sometimes in the same sentence

Many Hispanic customers in the US move between English and Spanish all day without thinking about it. They text in one, argue in the other, and count in whichever comes first. Their searches do the same.

Some searches go out in English: *roof repair near me*, *AC not cooling*. Others go out in Spanish: *abogado de accidentes*, *dentista para niños*. And a lot of them land somewhere in between:

- *roofer que hable español*
- *dentist que acepte Medicaid*
- *abogado de inmigración near me*

Notice which ones tend to switch to Spanish. Health. Legal trouble. Money. Family. The moments when a misunderstood word costs more than time.

> When the stakes rise, people reach for the language they feel safest in.

**What this means for you:** an English-only website is invisible to a whole share of these searches. A website that only *translates* its English pages is invisible to another share, because the Spanish searches aren't word-for-word copies of the English ones. They're different questions, asked in a different voice.

## They type the way they talk

Search boxes don't get formal Spanish. They get kitchen Spanish, truck Spanish, *ay-se-me-rompió-el-carro* Spanish.

That means:

- **Missing accents.** *Mecanico*, *cerca de mi*, *limpieza dental economica*. Google usually understands, but your page still needs to sound like the person searching, not like a textbook.
- **Regional words.** A truck may be a *troca* in Texas, a *camioneta* somewhere else. A car might be a *carro*, an *auto* or a *máquina*, depending on whether the family's roots are in Mexico, the Caribbean or South America. The same broken pipe calls for a *plomero* almost everywhere in the Americas — but if your content says *fontanero*, the word they use in Spain, it sounds like it came from across an ocean.
- **Whole questions.** *¿Cuánto cuesta arreglar un techo?* *¿Qué hago si me chocan y no tengo papeles del carro?* People ask Google the way they'd ask a neighbor over the fence, and Google rewards pages that answer that question plainly.

**What this means for you:** the words on your page should come from your customers' mouths, not from a translation tool. Know who lives in your area. Write the way they talk.

## The phone is the front door

Marisol isn't sitting at a desk. She's standing in a garage in her socks. For most of these searches, the whole journey — search, compare, decide, call — happens on a phone, often in under five minutes.

That small screen is unforgiving:

- If your page loads slowly, she's gone.
- If your phone number isn't a button she can tap, she's gone.
- If the only way to reach you is a long form, she's gone — probably to the business with a **WhatsApp** button, because for many Hispanic families WhatsApp isn't an app. It's the kitchen table where the whole family talks.

And very often, she never reaches your website at all. She decides from the **Google Maps** listing: your photos, your hours, your reviews, your "Call" button. Your Google Business Profile is not a side detail. For a local business, it's the storefront window.

## Reviews are read like letters from a neighbor

Stars matter. But Hispanic customers tend to read *past* the stars, looking for one thing: someone like them.

A single review in Spanish — *"Me explicaron todo en español y el precio fue el mismo que me dijeron"* — can do more than twenty five-star reviews that say "Great service!" It tells the reader: *people like me came here, and they were treated right.*

And when the owner answers that review in Spanish, something quiet but powerful happens. The business stops being a logo and starts being a person who will pick up the phone and understand.

**What this means for you:** ask your Spanish-speaking customers for reviews, in Spanish. Answer every one of them, in the language it was written in.

## "Que hable español" is a search all by itself

This may be the most important point of all: many people don't just search for a service. They search for the *language* as part of the service.

- [*dentista que hable español*](/dental/)
- *contador bilingüe*
- *mecánico hispano cerca de mí*

For these customers, being understood is not a bonus. It's the feature they're shopping for. If your team speaks Spanish but your website and Google listing never say so, you're hiding the one thing they're looking for.

## What to do this month

You don't need to rebuild your business. You need to become findable in the language your customers are already using. Start here:

1. **Say it out loud.** Add "Hablamos español" to your Google Business Profile, your website header and your service pages.
2. [**Write Spanish pages natively — don't translate.**](/sample/) One real page per main service, written around the searches people actually make.
3. **Post on Google in Spanish.** Short updates, offers, seasonal tips. They keep your listing alive and visible.
4. **Make contact effortless.** A tap-to-call button and a WhatsApp button on every page.
5. **Collect and answer reviews in Spanish.** They're the neighbor's word, and the neighbor's word still wins.

## Back in the garage

Marisol scrolled past three plumbers. One had no reviews. One had a website in perfect, cold English. One had a WhatsApp button and a review that began *"Muy amables, me explicaron todo…"*

She tapped the green button. Somebody answered.

That's the whole game. Not the biggest ad, not the fanciest website. Just being there, in her language, at a quarter to ten on a Tuesday night.
